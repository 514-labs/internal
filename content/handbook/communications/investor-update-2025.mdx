# Investor Update: 2025 Year in Review

If I had to pick a single word that best represents the past year it would be **focus**.

We started the year with bold ambitions. Ambitions of tackling multiple use cases with our products, ambitions of integrating the full data stack into a single framework, ambitions of building incredible and new AI products. In pursuit of those ambitions we built a diverse pipeline filled with companies of varying industries, sizes and stacks.

The scale of those ambitions, paired with our work ethic, hubris and tenacity turned into lots of code, lots of marketing materials, lots of sales activity and—most importantly—lots of learnings. The most important of which being that breadth of our offering was confusing, brittle and failed to capture the imagination of the most important user cohort that we have—**developers who want to build kickass data-intensive experiences into their apps but are operating at a scale where their existing transactional data systems aren't performing enough and they now need to move towards OLAP technologies.**

Hence, we spent the better part of the year focusing, simplifying, refactoring, all the while building on top of the incredible core technology that our engineering team had been building since the inception of the company and making sure that we meet users where they are.

## Narrowing ICP

<Badge variant="secondary">GTM Focus</Badge>

We started the year looking for teams that needed to build operational data warehouses or user facing analytics. Unfortunately, searching for these teams means you take meetings with companies like Amazon, Cox, Diageo, Charter, Nike, "those midmarket enterprises" and "that one startup in your investor's portfolio." As cool as these companies are to sell to, the problems they have don't all map to a single use case that we could execute around.

**Now, we focus on B2B SaaS companies whose core competency is NOT to deliver insights to their customers.** We'll also take meetings with B2C companies that have a key user facing analytics feature such as the biometric reports that F45 built on MooseStack but we are very selective with our outreach and approach to those companies.

### Case Study: Commvault

A good example for B2B SaaS companies is Commvault, they are a public backup management service. But like B2B SaaS companies, Commvault's users expect to be able to slice and dice their data. Commvault has had dashboarding and reporting features in their products for years. These dashboards have been built on OLTP technologies and take 45+ seconds to load.

We showed them that using MooseStack + ClickHouse they could create sub-second experiences for their users and ship their features entirely in code, while using their existing engineering best practices. We're now in the final stages of a POC where they are integrating their production environments with our managed hosting. All signs point to a successful outcome and we're excited to see where it all goes.

## OLAP in Your App

<Badge variant="secondary">Use Case Focus</Badge>

B2B SaaS companies and scaled B2C companies with a data-intensive feature live and die by the apps and services they ship to their users. What great apps look like is subjective but, for a given market, typically comes down to feature set, performance and quality. The experiences users have with other products and services heavily influences the quality/performance bar that must be hit for a given feature set. And the quality standards set for most users now is that things need to be fast, not just visually fast, but with AI, **performance must be conversationally-fast.**

And to hit that performance bar OLAP is the name of the game. Once B2B SaaS companies hit a certain level of data volumes (usually starting at ~1M records), most insights turn from transactional to analytical, requiring large table scans and multiple data sources to be truly valuable. OLAP systems were designed for this though, most were originally built for large scale data warehousing use cases. ClickHouse, on the other hand, was designed to scale from a developer's laptop to the large scale cloud and that's the main reason why MooseStack was designed with OLAP and ClickHouse at its heart.

## OLAP Module

<Badge variant="secondary">Product Focus</Badge>

We started the year with a bold bet. We decided to break a tightly coupled set of capabilities in Moose into separate modules that could be leveraged independently. We made this bet based on some early customer discovery work that showed users wanted to have different entry points into the product and based on some direct feedback we'd gotten from some of our design partners:

### What We Learned

It became clear as we worked with our own product and with users that we needed Moose to be lighter, easier to integrate into the existing development stack and more declarative (less auto magical). The modules were already essentially built into the tightly integrated framework and hence it made sense to break them into their own separate modules that users could derive value from.

**That turned out to be a mistake on two fronts:**

1. The increased surface area of the product meant more surface area for bugs
2. Users always started with the OLAP module—the thesis that they'd start with other modules turned out to be wrong

Instead of a pure modularization of the parts that could be used independently to derive value, users needed the framework to be **composable and integratable** into their stack.
